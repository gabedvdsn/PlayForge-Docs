Writing your own scaler

A scaler of your own derives from AbstractScaler, or from AbstractCachedScaler for an attribute set's values, and has a public constructor with no parameters. The Kind menu lists it by its name without "Scaler", as Damage Curve for DamageCurveScaler. Its inspector draws:

  • Each field it adds, public or [SerializeField], as Unity draws the field's type, through any property drawer you write for that type. The fields of an abstract base of your own draw too. [HideInInspector] keeps a field out, and [ForgeShowWhen(nameof(Mode), EMode.Curved)] shows one only while another field, an enum or a bool, holds one of the values given.
  • The parts every scaler has, as its Parts say: its values per level (EScalerParts.LevelValues), the level range they spread over (EScalerParts.LevelRange), and its behaviours (EScalerParts.Behaviours). Unless you override it, a scaler reads all three, or its behaviours alone where its UseScalingOptions() returns false. Override Parts to say what yours reads, as a scaler that reads only an attribute returns EScalerParts.Behaviours. Parts can follow the scaler's own fields, as one that reads its values per level in one mode alone.

A kind of number of your own is simpler: derive from NumberKind and override its one method, Evaluate(in NumberContext context), which reads what the field's site has: context.Caster, context.Target, context.Level, the caller's magnitudes, Local Data, an attribute on either side, and more; context.Has(...) says which. Describe() gives the field's summary, as "the distance to the target". [NumberKind("Distance to Target", Reads = NumberReads.Caster | NumberReads.Target)] names it and says what it reads, so a field offers it only where it can be worked out; where it can't, the field reads its number. [ForgeMenu("Space")] files it in the kind menu. A kind is a scaler, so it also works in an effect's magnitude and inside a Group or a Conditional, and its fields draw as a scaler's do. Test one without an effect: number.Evaluate(NumberContext.For(caster, target, level)). A kind is saved by its class's name, so renaming the class needs Unity's [MovedFrom]. PlayForge's own kinds, Local Data, Tracked Impact, Asset Part and the rest, are written this way, in Runtime/PlayForge/Numbers/Kinds.

A condition of your own, for a Conditional, is the same: derive from GameplayCondition, override Evaluate(in NumberContext context) to say whether it holds and Describe() for its line, and name it with [GameplayCondition("Target Is Caster", Reads = NumberReads.Caster | NumberReads.Target)]; the condition menu offers it, and condition.Holds(context) reads it. A kind or a condition that holds numbers reads them with itself as their owner, MaxValue.Evaluate(context, this), and passes Initialize(carrier) on to them, so a number in it captures what it captures as an effect's spec is made. PlayForge's are in Runtime/PlayForge/Numbers/Conditions.
